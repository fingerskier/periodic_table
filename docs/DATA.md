# Element data

`src/data/elements.json` holds one record per element (Z = 1–118) in the shape of `ElementData`
(`src/types.ts`). It is generated by `scripts/build_data.py` from the database bundled with the
[mendeleev](https://github.com/lmmentel/mendeleev) Python package, version 1.3.0, plus a small set of
documented corrections. The JSON is committed, so CI and the web build need no Python.

```sh
python3 -m venv .venv && .venv/bin/pip install mendeleev==1.3.0 && .venv/bin/python scripts/build_data.py
.venv/bin/python scripts/build_data.py --check            # fails if the committed JSON is stale
.venv/bin/python scripts/build_data.py --compare PATH     # cross-check against pymatgen (see below)
```

The script reads `elements.db` with `sqlite3` only (mendeleev is imported just to find the file),
refuses other mendeleev versions, and writes deterministic output: records sorted by Z, keys in
contract order, `json.dump(indent=1, ensure_ascii=False)` plus a trailing newline. Re-running yields
byte-identical JSON. It also validates the result (118 rows, categories, the La–Lu and Ac–Lr series,
the 11 gases and 2 liquids, note length, an oxidation-state entry for every element) before writing.
`tests/data.test.ts` checks the committed file.

### Choices made when mapping fields

- **Allotropes.** When the phase-transition table has several rows, the conventional standard-state
  form is used: white phosphorus (as CRC lists it), monoclinic sulfur for `mp` (rhombic sulfur, the
  standard state, converts to it at 368.35 K; `density` is for rhombic sulfur), grey selenium, white
  tin (grey tin converts at 286.35 K). Graphite (C) and grey arsenic each have a single row whose
  boiling point is a sublimation point. Arsenic's stored "melting point" is its triple point
  (1090 K at 3.7 MPa) and mendeleev stores none for carbon, so `mp` is null for both. Helium has no
  `mp` because it does not solidify at 1 atm.
- **Phase.** `gas` if `bp` < 298.15 K; `liquid` if `mp` < 298.15 K ≤ `bp`; `solid` if `mp` ≥ 298.15 K,
  or if the element sublimes above 298.15 K; otherwise null. This yields 11 gases (H, He, N, O, F, Ne,
  Cl, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) and 2 liquids (Br, Hg); Cs (301.65 K) and Ga (302.9146 K) are solids. Francium's
  estimated melting point lies near room temperature and it has no boiling point, so its phase is null.
- **Van der Waals radii.** Alvarez (2013) is derived from the distribution of non-bonded contact
  distances in the Cambridge Structural Database and covers 94 elements here (H to Es, except Pm, Po,
  At, Fr, Ra). Bondi (1964) and Mantina et al. (2009) cover only the main group. Alvarez's radii for
  metals are noticeably larger than older Bondi-type values (Na 250 pm against 227 pm).
- **Bismuth** is counted as not radioactive: ²⁰⁹Bi alpha-decays with a half-life of about 2×10¹⁹ years
  (P. de Marcillac et al., "Experimental detection of α-particles from the radioactive decay of natural
  bismuth", *Nature* 422 (2003) 876), so for all practical purposes it is stable. A note says so.

## Categories

Nine metallicity classes (`CategoryId`). Halogens are a group, not a class, so they fold into the
reactive nonmetals; group names live in the column data. mendeleev's series map as follows:

| mendeleev series | Category |
| --- | --- |
| Alkali metals | `alkali-metal` (Li–Fr) |
| Alkaline earth metals | `alkaline-earth-metal` (Be–Ra) |
| Transition metals | `transition-metal` (groups 3–12; Lu and Lr excepted) |
| Poor metals | `post-transition-metal` |
| Metalloids | `metalloid` (Po excepted) |
| Nonmetals, Halogens | `reactive-nonmetal` (At and Ts excepted) |
| Noble gases | `noble-gas` |
| Lanthanides, Actinides | `lanthanide`, `actinide` |

The result: 38 transition metals, 15 lanthanides, 15 actinides, 13 post-transition metals (Al, Ga, In,
Sn, Tl, Pb, Bi, Po, Nh, Fl, Mc, Lv, Ts), 11 reactive nonmetals (H, C, N, O, F, P, S, Cl, Se, Br, I),
7 metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, At), 7 noble gases, 6 alkali metals, 6 alkaline earth metals.

Contested boundaries and the choices made:

- **Lanthanides and actinides.** IUPAC's Red Book (*Nomenclature of Inorganic Chemistry*, 2005,
  IR-3.5) defines the lanthanoids as La–Lu and the actinoids as Ac–Lr: 15 each. mendeleev files Lu
  and Lr as transition metals; here they are a lanthanide and an actinide. The category does not
  change with the group-3 convention shown on the cylinder (Sc-Y-Lu-Lr or Sc-Y-La-Ac).
- **Group 12.** Zn, Cd, Hg (and Cn) are counted as transition metals. IR-3.5 notes that the d-block
  elements of groups 3–12 are commonly called transition elements, though group 12 is not always
  included; some tables class them as post-transition metals.
- **Metalloids.** No IUPAC definition exists. R. E. Vernon, "Which elements are metalloids?",
  *J. Chem. Educ.* 90 (2013) 1703–1707 (doi:[10.1021/ed3008457](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed3008457)),
  finds six commonly recognised metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) and five less often included
  (C, Al, Se, Po, At). Here C and Se are reactive nonmetals and Al is a post-transition metal.
- **Polonium** is a post-transition metal: the solid is a metallic conductor (simple cubic α-Po) and
  forms Po²⁺/Po⁴⁺ salts. mendeleev lists it as a metalloid. See also S. J. Hawkes, "Polonium and
  astatine are not semimetals", *J. Chem. Educ.* 87 (2010) 783, doi:[10.1021/ed100308w](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed100308w).
- **Astatine** is classed as a metalloid, with `category` marked predicted. Tracer-scale chemistry
  shows both halogen-like (At⁻) and metal-like, cationic behaviour (At⁺); bulk astatine has never been
  made, and relativistic DFT predicts condensed astatine to be a monatomic fcc metal (J. Hermann,
  R. Hoffmann and N. W. Ashcroft, "Condensed astatine: monatomic and metallic", *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 111
  (2013) 116404, doi:[10.1103/PhysRevLett.111.116404](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.111.116404)).
  "Metalloid" records the intermediate chemistry that many tables show; if the prediction is
  confirmed, astatine would be a post-transition metal.
- **Superheavy elements.** Rf–Hs have single-atom chemistry confirming group 4–8 behaviour (e.g. BhO₃Cl:
  R. Eichler et al., *Nature* 407 (2000) 63; HsO₄: Ch. E. Düllmann et al., *Nature* 418 (2002) 859).
  From Mt (Z = 109) on, chemistry is either unstudied (Mt, Ds, Rg, Lv, Ts, Og) or limited to
  single-atom adsorption experiments that do not settle metallic character (Cn: R. Eichler et al.,
  *Nature* 447 (2007) 72; Fl: A. Yakushev et al., *Inorg. Chem.* 53 (2014) 1624,
  doi:[10.1021/ic4026766](https://doi.org/10.1021/ic4026766); Nh and Mc: A. Yakushev et al.,
  *Front. Chem.* 12 (2024) 1474820, doi:[10.3389/fchem.2024.1474820](https://doi.org/10.3389/fchem.2024.1474820)).
  So `category` is marked predicted for Z ≥ 109. Their classes follow their groups (Mt–Cn
  transition metals, Nh–Lv post-transition metals, Og noble gas), except **tennessine**, classed as a
  post-transition metal. Ts has never been studied chemically; metallic character rises down group 17
  (astatine is already predicted to be metallic) and relativistic effects on the 7p shell weaken
  halogen-like behaviour further, so a metal is the likelier class. Oganesson stays in `noble-gas` by group, with a note that it is
  predicted to be a semiconducting solid (O. R. Smits et al., *Angew. Chem. Int. Ed.* 59 (2020) 23636,
  doi:[10.1002/anie.202011976](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202011976)).
- **Hydrogen** is a reactive nonmetal and **helium** a noble gas, whatever their table position.

Einsteinium's density (8.84 g/cm³) is not marked predicted: it is the X-ray density of fcc ²⁵³Es metal
(a = 575 pm; R. G. Haire and R. D. Baybarz, "Studies of einsteinium metal", *J. Phys. Colloq.* 40 (1979)
C4-101, doi:[10.1051/jphyscol:1979431](https://doi.org/10.1051/jphyscol:1979431)), although
mendeleev's metadata lists it among estimates. `mass` is never marked predicted: a mass number is
exact, and `massIsMassNumber` already says the value is not an atomic weight.

## Oxidation states

`oxidationStates` lists the oxidation states an element commonly shows in its compounds: the short
list a periodic table prints, not every state ever observed. The inspector labels it "Oxidation
states", and readers take it as the common ones. mendeleev's `main` list (the states printed in bold in
Wikipedia's oxidation-state table) fell short of that: copper had only +2, gold only +3, uranium only
+6, plutonium only +4, rhenium only +4, and tin and germanium were given −4. It is no longer used.
`scripts/build_data.py` holds an explicit table, `OXIDATION_STATES`, with an entry for each of the 118
elements and the reason for every departure from the reference list below.

**Policy.**

- *Reference list:* the oxidation states printed on the CRC Handbook's periodic table ("Periodic
  Table of the Elements", *CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics*). The printed Handbook was not to
  hand for this revision, so the values were read from periodic-table.org's element pages (Cu +1 +2,
  Mn +2 +3 +4 +7, Re +4 +6 +7, Os +3 +4, U to Am +3 +4 +5 +6, Kr and Xe 0) and compared with PubChem's
  periodic table, a second reproduction that has the same lists for all but six elements up to Lr
  (listed after the departures table). Neither copies the Handbook everywhere: for C and Si,
  periodic-table.org gives every state from −4 to +4, Wikipedia's full list rather than a periodic-table
  cell's. Both should be checked against a printed copy.
- *Listed:* a state in which the element has a substantial chemistry: its common minerals, stable
  binary compounds (oxides, halides, hydrides, chalcogenides), ions in water, or widely used reagents
  and catalysts.
- *Not listed:* 0 (the element itself); states known from only a few compounds or under special
  conditions (matrix isolation, the gas phase, strongly alkaline oxidising solution); the low formal
  states of carbonyl and organometallic complexes. Si(II) (SiO, SiF₂), which exists only at high
  temperature or as transient species, is therefore absent, as are Pr(IV), Tb(IV), Np(VII), Pu(VII)
  and Xe(VIII).
- *Checks:* mendeleev's `main` list; pymatgen's `ICSD oxidation states` (the states pymatgen assigns
  to the element in compounds of the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database); N. N. Greenwood and
  A. Earnshaw, *Chemistry of the Elements*, 2nd ed., Butterworth-Heinemann (1997) for the chemistry
  behind each departure; L. R. Morss, N. M. Edelstein and J. Fuger (eds.), *The Chemistry of the
  Actinide and Transactinide Elements*, 3rd ed., Springer (2006) for the actinides. The build fails if
  an element has no entry or if a listed state is absent from the database's `main` and `extended`
  lists (a guard against typos).
- *Superheavy elements:* the state shown by single-atom chemistry, for Rf–Hs only, with a note naming
  the experiment. No compound chemistry has been established from Mt onward (Mt–Rg, Lv, Ts and Og have
  not been studied chemically; Cn, Nh, Fl and Mc only in adsorption experiments on the atoms), so their
  lists are empty, as are those of He, Ne, Ar and Rn.

**Where the two reproductions differ.** PubChem's periodic table (National Center for Biotechnology
Information, <https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/periodic-table/>; its 2022 data as packaged in the npm
module `pubchem-elements` 1.0.0) has the same lists as periodic-table.org (as read for this table) for
every element up to Lr except these six; the dataset's choice for each:

- C and Si: PubChem +2, +4, −4. Carbon's list is the dataset's; silicon's +2 is left out (see *Not
  listed* above).
- Br: PubChem −1, +1, +5. The dataset keeps +3 (BrF₃, the bromites), as periodic-table.org and the
  `main` list do and as for Cl and I.
- Tc: PubChem +4, +6, +7. Tc(VI) is left out, as on periodic-table.org and in the `main` list: it
  has few compounds (TcF₆ and some oxo- and nitridohalides).
- Pd: PubChem +2, +3. The dataset has +2, +4, as periodic-table.org and the `main` list do: Pd(IV) is
  well established ([PdCl₆]²⁻ salts, PdF₄), Pd(III) is rare.
- At: PubChem −1, +1, +3, +5, +7. The dataset has the `main` list's −1 and +1 (all astatine

**Against pymatgen's ICSD list** (75 elements have one; `--compare` prints the comparison): every

## Cross-check against pymatgen

`scripts/build_data.py --compare periodic_table.json` compares the final dataset with pymatgen's
`core/periodic_table.json` (extracted from the pymatgen-core 2026.10.16 sdist), reporting differences
above these tolerances: mass 0.1%, Pauling electronegativity (`X`) 0.05, `mp`/`bp` 10 K, density 3%
(`Density of solid`, kg/m³ → g/cm³), atomic radius 0.5 pm, first ionization energy 0.05 eV (pymatgen's
list is in eV although its unit table says kJ/mol), electron affinity 0.02 eV. It also prints, per
element, how `oxidationStates` differs from mendeleev's `main` list and from pymatgen's ICSD list (see
[Oxidation states](#oxidation-states)). Every numeric discrepancy was resolved as follows.

**Mass.** After the corrections above, four differences remain; ours are kept.

| Element | Ours | pymatgen | Resolution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tc | [97] | 98 | Tie on CIAAW's list, see above |
| Po | [209] | 210 | ²⁰⁹Po (124 yr) outlives ²¹⁰Po (138 d) |
| Rn | [222] | 220 | ²²²Rn (3.82 d) outlives ²²⁰Rn (56 s) |
| Lr | [266] | 262 | ²⁶⁶Lr (22 h) is on CIAAW's current list |

Before the corrections pymatgen also differed for Sg, Bh, Hs, Mt, Rg and Mc; it agrees with the new
values. Its standard atomic weights are older still (e.g. Li 6.941, S 32.065), within tolerance.

**Electronegativity.** Kept as CRC. mendeleev's values are exactly the CRC column of Wikipedia's
"Electronegativities of the elements (data page)"; pymatgen follows WebElements, which mixes in
A. L. Allred's oxidation-state-specific values (*J. Inorg. Nucl. Chem.* 17 (1961) 215,
doi:[10.1016/0022-1902(61)80142-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1902(61)80142-5)). Differences:
Tc 2.10/1.9, Lu 1.0/1.27, W 1.7/2.36, Pt 2.2/2.28, Au 2.4/2.54, Hg 1.9/2.00, Tl 1.8/1.62, Pb 1.8/2.33,
Bi 1.9/2.02, U 1.7/1.38, Np 1.3/1.36 (ours/pymatgen). Pauling himself gives 2.4 for gold (*The
Nature of the Chemical Bond*, 3rd ed., 1960). pymatgen also has values where CRC has none (Kr 3.0,
Rn 2.2, Pm 1.13, Eu 1.2, Tb 1.1, Yb 1.1, Am–Lr 1.3); these are WebElements estimates and stay null.

**Melting points.** He, C and As have no `mp` here by design (pymatgen's He 0.95 K is under pressure;
its C 3800 K and As 1090 K are not at 1 atm). Five differences keep the CRC 97th-edition values,
mendeleev's documented and critically evaluated source: Pm 1315/1373, Ho 1745/1734, Lu 1936/1925,
Ir 2719/2739, Th 2023/2115 K (ours/pymatgen).

**Boiling points.** Eighteen differences keep the CRC values for the same reason: B
4273/4200, C 4098 (sublimation)/4300, Si 3538/3173, Cu 2833/3200, Ga 2502/2477, Ge 3106/3093, In
2300/2345, Sn 2859/2875, Ba 2118/2143, Ce 3716/3633, Pr 3793/3563, Nd 3347/3373, Gd 3546/3523, Ho
2973/2993, Au 3109/3129, Ac 3473/3573, Th 5058/5093, U 4404/4200 K. Boiling points of refractory
metals and lanthanides are uncertain by tens of kelvin or more: Y. Zhang, J. R. G. Evans and S. Yang,
"Corrected values for boiling points and enthalpies of vaporization of elements in handbooks",
*J. Chem. Eng. Data* 56 (2011) 328, doi:[10.1021/je1011086](https://doi.org/10.1021/je1011086),
propose corrected values that differ from the CRC's by up to a few hundred kelvin for some elements
(W 6203 K against 5828 K, Au 3243 K against 3109 K), and Wikipedia quotes theirs. The CRC values are
kept as the documented source; compare boiling points of such elements with that spread in mind.
pymatgen's values for Pm, Ra, Np, Am and Cm, absent from the CRC table, are not adopted.

**Density.** Ours kept in each case: B 2.34/2.46 (β-rhombohedral, the stable form, against
α-boron), C 2.2/2.267 (CRC's bulk graphite against the ideal crystal), S 2.07/1.96 (rhombic, the
standard state, against monoclinic), Ba 3.62/3.51 (3.62 agrees with the X-ray density from the bcc
lattice, about 3.6), Yb 6.90/6.57 (the fcc lattice gives about 6.97, so 6.57 is inconsistent), Ra
5.5/5.0 (our X-ray correction above). K and Tc now agree with pymatgen after the corrections. pymatgen
has no gas or liquid densities and none for At, Am, Es, Fm, Md, No.

**Atomic radius.** Only Ar differs: pymatgen keeps 71 pm, removed here (see above).

**First ionization energy.** No differences above 0.05 eV. pymatgen's values for Mt (50 eV) and Ds
(65 eV) are not ionization energies of neutral atoms and are ignored.

**Electron affinity.** After the corrections above (Os now agrees: 1.0778/1.0777), two differences
remain. Ce 0.65/0.600 keeps the CRC value: tabulated values range from about 0.57 to 0.65 eV, so
pymatgen's is not clearly better. Og 0.056/0.081 are both calculations; ours is marked predicted.
pymatgen also gives negative estimates for atoms without a bound anion (He, Be, Ne, Mg, Ar, Mn, Zn,
Kr, Cd, Xe, Hg, Rn), values for Pm, Sm, Gd, Dy, Ho, Er, Po, Pa, Np–Lr and Rg–Ts that are mostly
calculations, and 0.315 eV for U. None is adopted: pymatgen names no source for them and they could
not be checked here. For Sc, Ti, Cr, Fe, Sr and Zr pymatgen has more precise values that differ from
the CRC ones by less than 0.02 eV; the CRC values are kept.

## Limitations

- Pauling electronegativities for heavy elements depend on the compilation (see above); compare
  trends, not second decimals.
- Discovery years compress contested histories into one number; notes flag the main disputes.
- Oxidation states are the common ones only, by design; where references disagree on what counts as
  common, [Oxidation states](#oxidation-states) gives the choice made, and the reference list was read
  from two reproductions of the CRC table, which differ for six elements, rather than the printed Handbook.
- Superheavy entries (Z ≥ 104) carry mass numbers, configurations from calculations, a few
  calculated ionization energies, and little else, by design.
